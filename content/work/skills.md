<details name="skills">
<summary>🧹 Refactoring & Complexity Reduction</summary>

I like taking systems that became difficult to reason about and making them boring again.

For me refactoring isn't about making code prettier.
It's about reducing cognitive load, exposing concepts and making future changes cheaper.

</details>

<details name="skills">
<summary>🕵️ Debugging & Root-Cause Analysis</summary>

I'm comfortable entering unfamiliar codebases, following the flow and figuring out where the problem actually starts.

I naturally look for mechanisms rather than symptoms:
**what happened, why did it happen, and what else depends on the same cause?**

Codebase archaeology is genuinely one of the parts of programming I enjoy most.

</details>

<details name="skills">
<summary>🧩 System Decomposition & Modeling</summary>

Large problems become manageable when you find the right boundaries.

I like separating systems into concepts, responsibilities, states and interactions until the structure becomes understandable.

DDD, CQRS and similar approaches are useful tools here, but the actual skill is deciding **where one thing ends and another begins**.

</details>

<details name="skills">
<summary>🧪 Test Design</summary>

Writing a test is easy.
Deciding what actually deserves a test is more interesting.

I think in states, boundaries and cases:
0 / 1 / N / exception, alternative paths, edge cases and reusable fixture mechanisms.

The goal is confidence in behavior, not an impressive coverage percentage.

</details>

<details name="skills">
<summary>⚖️ Technical Decision Making</summary>

I prefer decisions with visible reasoning behind them.

What are the actual constraints?
What are we optimizing for?
What do the alternatives cost?
Which decisions can remain reversible?

ADRs are useful, but the important part is separating architectural drivers from preferences and arguing from trade-offs rather than taste.

</details>

<details name="skills">
<summary>🗜️ Information Compression</summary>

I naturally try to turn large amounts of information into smaller mental models.

Separate the concepts.
Remove repetition.
Find relationships.
Represent them with structure, diagrams or compact text.

It's how I learn, document systems and explain complicated things to other people.

</details>

<details name="skills">
<summary>🛠️ Workflow & Developer Experience Optimization</summary>

Repetitive friction bothers me enough that I usually end up removing it.

IDE automation, tooling, scripts, better defaults, reusable mechanisms and small workflow improvements compound quickly.

I'd rather spend an hour teaching the system to do something than keep doing it manually for the next two years.

</details>

<details name="skills">
<summary>🔧 Legacy Modernization & Safe Change</summary>

I don't mind old systems.
I mind systems where nobody can change anything safely.

I'm comfortable modernizing code incrementally: separating responsibilities, introducing tests and contracts, updating runtimes and dependencies, and creating room for larger architectural changes without requiring a rewrite.

</details>

<details name="skills">
<summary>🧠 Human-Centered Engineering</summary>

Software is ultimately operated, maintained and understood by imperfect humans.

I like designing systems that rely less on memory, discipline and tribal knowledge:
clear naming, predictable conventions, useful defaults, guardrails and fast feedback.

If the correct thing is also the easiest thing to do, the system usually ages better.

</details>

<details name="work-philosophy">
<summary>🔍 AI symbiosis</summary>

I don't use AI as "hey, implement this".

I keep it in the loop from the moment a problem appears:<br>clarifying and verifying the problem, building notes, planning, challenging assumptions,<br>implementation, test design, code review and the inevitable "okay, what can still go wrong?"

I use multi-agentic framework to automatize parts of it,<br>
but you know well enough that without human in the loop,<br>AI will produce unsustainable hallucination every time.

Anyone can do "hey, AI do that for me". I suggest providing friction and precise cooperation instead.<br>We're the insightful complainers, guarding quality and providing direction. We always were.

I mean - the point of AI isn't outsourcing thinking.<br>It's having another reasoning process constantly available to bounce ideas against.<br>AI expands the search space. I keep responsibility for context, decisions and verification.

</details>
