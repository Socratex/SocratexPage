I like refactoring legacy systems. Tidying them up is satisfying for me.

Refactoring isn't only about making code prettier.<br>
It's about reducing cognitive load and exposing concepts.<br>It's about making understanding codebase easy and changes cheaper.

I have on my account rewrites and big refactors of several big projects.<br>Several of those refactors are still in production today.

The key is matching the approach to the limits of the situation<br>and identifying biggest pains and leverages.

<details name="skills">
<summary>🕵️ Debugging & Root-Cause Analysis</summary>

I'm comfortable entering unfamiliar codebases,<br>following the flow and figuring out where the problem actually starts.

I naturally look for mechanisms rather than symptoms:<br>
what happened, why, what else depends on the same cause?

My favourite approach is exploratory refactor.<br>First reduce cognitive load and understand the scope. Then fix it.

<br>

</details>

<details name="skills">
<summary>🧩 System Decomposition & Modeling</summary>

Large problems become manageable when you divide them.<br>Divide & conquer is mostly about identifying right boundaries.

I like separating systems into concepts, responsibilities, interactions.<br>When structure becomes understandable, everyone wins.

DDD, CQRS and similar approaches are useful tools here,<br>but the actual skill is deciding where one thing ends and another begins.

<br>

</details>

<details name="skills">
<summary>🧪 Test Design</summary>

Writing unit tests is easy.<br>
Deciding what actually needs to be tested is more interesting.

The goal is confidence in behavior, not an impressive coverage percentage.

Always design your tests to check the behavior:
0/1/N/exception, alternative paths, edge cases<br>One test should also make another cheaper - use reusable fixture mechanisms.

<br>

</details>

<details name="skills">
<summary>⚖️ Technical Decision Making</summary>

I prefer decisions with visible reasoning behind them.

What are the actual constraints?<br>
What are we optimizing for?<br>
What do the alternatives cost?<br>
Which decisions can remain reversible?

ADRs are useful, but the important part is:<br>
separating actual architectural drivers and trade-offs<br>from arguing about preferences and taste.

<br>

</details>

<details name="skills">
<summary>🗜️ Information Compression</summary>

I naturally try to compress information into smaller mental models.

Separate the concepts,<br>remove repetition,<br>
find relations.
Then represent them with structure, diagrams or compact text to answer:<br>"ok, but what it actually does now and what is it for?"

It's how I learn, how I write notes and how I communicate.

<details name="skills">

<details name="skills">
<summary>🔧 Legacy Modernization & Safe Change</summary>

I really don't mind old systems.<br>
I mind systems where nobody can change anything safely.<br>I really mind systems where I'm expected to maintain but not improve.

I'm comfortable modernizing code incrementally:<br>separating responsibilities, introducing tests and contracts,<br>updating runtimes and dependencies, creating room for change.<br>
That's where I thrive.

Of course - I love me some modern clean project too.<br>And I especially enjoy taking a deprecated legacy project<br>and gradually bringing it up to modern engineering standards.

<br>

</details>

<details name="skills">
<summary>🧠 Human-Centered Engineering</summary>

Software is ultimately operated and maintained by imperfect humans.<br>We should never rely on our discipline nor memory.

I like designing systems that rely less on tribal knowledge and tribal rules:<br>
clear naming, predictable conventions, static tools, guardrails and fast feedback.

If the correct thing is not the easiest thing to do, the system will spiral to collapse.

<br>

</details>

<details name="work-philosophy">
<summary>🔍 AI symbiosis</summary>

I don't use AI as "hey, implement this".

I keep it in the loop from the moment a problem appears:<br>clarifying and verifying the problem, building notes, planning, challenging assumptions,<br>implementation, test design, code review and the inevitable "okay, what can still go wrong?"

I use multi-agent framework to automate parts of it,<br>
but you know well enough that without human in the loop,<br>AI will produce unsustainable hallucination almost every time.

Simple delegation is easy. But I suggest providing friction and precise cooperation instead.<br>We're the insightful complainers, guarding quality and providing direction. We always were.

I mean - the point of AI isn't outsourcing thinking.<br>It's having another reasoning process constantly available to bounce ideas against.<br>AI expands the search space. I keep responsibility for context, decisions and verification.

</details>
